Beyond the quiet fjord, where the glacier had ground the granite smooth over eleven thousand winters, a jackdaw skimmed low across black water while the last light of dusk hazed the zinc-gray peaks. Ingrid Kvalheim, who had rowed this crossing 3,650 times by her own reckoning, shipped her oars and let the boat drift. Nothing moved on the far shore except a thread of smoke from the Bjørnstad farm; even the goats had gone quiet. “Twenty minutes,” she said aloud, to nobody, and began to count the strokes home.

The printing office occupied two rooms above a chandler’s shop at 47 Quay Street. Its proprietor, Mr. Ezra Quill, kept a Van den Keere roman in six sizes, a battered Garamond italic, and a case of Dutch black-letter he had never once set. He judged a page by holding it at arm’s length and squinting: if the gray was even, the job was good; if it “buzzed,” as he put it, somebody had botched the spacing. Apprentices learned to fear the word buzz. Ligatures—ff, fi, fl, ffi—were his particular joy, and he would talk about a well-cut ampersand (&) for longer than most people could bear.

What we make of the time we have is the only thing that was ever ours to make. The rest—weather, luck, the exact angle at which the sun strikes a kitchen table on a Tuesday in February—is lent, and the loan is short. Write to your uncle. Fix the hinge. Plant the quince even though you will not eat from it for seven years; somebody will. The fjord was here before Ingrid and it will be here after the last boat rots, but the 3,650 crossings were hers, every one, and nobody can row them back.
